# Architecture

Cape Sorrow is a game on [`@csarkosh/engine`](https://github.com/csarkosh/engine), which draws the world, runs the
netcode and the deterministic simulation, and owns the page. The game gives the engine's shell one page definition and
one identity, and lays two things of its own over the engine's world: a generated port town, and the title page. The
game's rules are still the engine's walking and nothing else. This file says what is true of the repository today, in
the order the pieces depend on each other: the engine and what the game hands it, the town, the title page, hosting, and
what is still scaffold and unfinished.

## The engine and what the game hands it

The game installs the engine as a release's tarball (the address in `client/package.json`; it is on v0.2.13). The engine
makes the world from a seed: the coast (the engine's `olympic` terrain, with its cove, its highway and its massif), the
forest and the clutter on it, the weather and the sky. A game registers the coast with a place laid over it, and the
world is then the engine's coast with that place's ground.

`client/src/main.ts` registers the shipped files and the terrain (`terrain.ts`, which imports `coast.ts`, which registers
the coast with `CAPE_PLACE`: see "The ground the game lays over the coast"), then calls the engine's `startShell` with
`CAPE_PAGE` (`client/src/page.ts`), a `PageGame`:

- `game`: the game a session is of (`EMPTY_GAME`, `client/src/game.ts`). Its renderer is the engine's, made with the town's renderer part (`townParts`), its start is the town's (`townStart`), and its rules (`WALK_RULES`, `client/src/sim.ts`) are the engine's walking, with two things set: `constrain` is the town's buildings as walls, and `spawn` searches out from the town's start for the first place a hull stands free.
- `side`: the game's own of the page (`createCapePage`), which the shell calls at the page's own points: it shows the title page, repaints it, shows a panel by its address, and keeps the mode. Nothing plays before a session starts.
- `identity` (`client/src/identity.ts`): the game's name, its word for a session (`case`), what a nameless player is called (`Detective`), and the prefix of everything it keeps in the browser (`capesorrow`).
- `away`: what the page for a visitor whose browser cannot play shows of the game: no still, and the same credits the Credits screen shows.
- `protocol`, `worldWorker`, `statusLine`, `scene`, `probeState`: the session's wire version, the worker that makes its world (`world.worker.ts`, handed the town's start and the chunk cells its first spawn reads, `townSpawnCells`), a line of status, the staged scenes (none), and the state the tier check measures the renderer on.

## The town

The town is simulation code in `client/src/sim/town/`: every machine computes the same town from the world's seed, with
nothing sent over the wire. The folder's rules (the arithmetic allowed, no trigonometry, bearings as triples, one random
stream per stage, `TOWN_GEN`) are in `AGENTS.md`, and `client/test/architecture.test.ts` and `client/test/town/banned.test.ts`
enforce them.

### The site

A plan is made from a `TownSite` (`site.ts`) and nothing else of the ground: the town's own seed (the world's, mixed with
which town this is), an anchor on the highway's centre line, and, in the town's own frame (origin at the anchor, +x
inland, +z north, metres), the shoreline's x and the highway's x sampled every 16 m along z, 1008 m either way of the
anchor, how far inland of the highway the town may reach (480 m), and a grid of the coast's own heights (8 m apart, out to 1.1 km inland and along the shore) that roads out of town are routed over and the land is judged on. The samples reach 1008 m either way of the anchor (the town's blocks reach 600 m, its outlying buildings further). `siteFor(worldSeed)` (`siteFor.ts`) reads it off
the engine's coast at the cove: the highway's line, and for each sample the shore found by a seaward scan from the highway
and then a fixed number of bisection steps, so every machine finds the same bits. It reads the coast's own ground, as it is
without a town laid over it (`coastGround`), so the site does not move when the town's ground is laid. Where a headland stands
at a sample the shore is the headland's tip; where the cove opens it is the cove's. There is one site, at the engine's cove. While the site is read the cliff bands are kept off a generous rectangle, so that the grid is the ground without them, as the roads out and the made ground lay it.

**The land** (`land.ts`) is derived once per site from the grid: for each cell its height and slope and whether it is buildable (no steeper than 14 percent, above 0.5 m, off the highway's bed, off the railroad's corridor, not on a headland's tongue), and the buildable hectares within the town's reach. The coast's own flats between the water and the highway stand 0 to 2 m, and the made ground lifts them to 2.5 m at the water's edge, so the height rule tests only that a cell is land and not tideflat awash. A site with no grid (the synthetic coast) has a flat land. Reading the site takes about 45 ms on a developer's machine and the land about 1 ms.

### The plan

`planTown(site, kind)` (`plan.ts`) returns a plain-data `TownPlan`: the history, the plats and the seam between them, the
streets and their ends, the roads out of town, blocks, lots, buildings, the landmarks, the made ground (`fill`), the `quay` and the `spawn`. The stages run in a
fixed order, each on a stream of its own: names, history, plats and streets, lots, buildings, landmarks. The history is drawn
first (`port/history.ts`) and every part of the form is derived from it, so that a difference between two towns has a cause a
player can name. `planFor(worldSeed)` (`planFor.ts`) is the plan on the world's own site, cached. A kind of town is a recipe
(`recipes.ts`) and a folder beside `port/`; the shared code never asks the kind, and only the large port exists. `TOWN_GEN` is
the generator's version (11) and is part of every plan.

On the real coast the shore runs near north to south, the highway stands 70 to 270 m inland of it, and at the cove two narrow
headlands run out to sea, so the cove is a natural harbour. The old plat stands at the cove behind a straight quay, partly on
made ground; the land company's compass plat wraps around it; the mill and the public port's terminal take the flats' shore;
the highway, which the engine paints and the town does not draw over, runs through, and blocks keep clear of it. A plan has 20 to
55 blocks and 150 to 420 buildings (`client/test/town/size.test.ts`): the land and the history's budget set it, as the next section says.

**The town is laid where the land allows, outward from where it began** (`port/layout.ts`, `port/growth.ts`). The old plat stands at the best landing on the cove (of the places along it a window of columns can stand with water for a quay, the one with most buildable land behind it), to the bearing the history gives it, but only as many of its blocks as the land takes (a block needs 60 percent of its ground buildable, a mean slope of 14 percent or less, and its mean height within 4.5 m of the graded plane the made ground will be: where the land takes fewer than four, the four best stand). The history gives the town a SIZE, a budget of blocks: 28, and up to 14 more for how long the town had to grow (a founding in 1889 adds none, one in 1851 all), 6 and up to 4 more where the boom was not cut short by the panic of 1893 (a peak in 1900 adds none, 1912 all), and 3 for a county seat, clamped to 26 to 55. The company's grid is laid over the whole of the land the town could reach (600 m either way along the shore, 480 m inland of the highway), and the budget is spent on its blocks one at a time, always on the cheapest next to the town: a cell's price is its distance on foot from the core (along the blocks taken, in metres), 8 m for each percent of its mean slope, 120 m for the whole of it being unbuildable, less up to 45 m for standing on the highway (falling to nothing 70 m off it) and up to 30 m for standing on the water (60 m). A cell less than 60 percent buildable, steeper than 14 percent, or off the graded plane by more than 4.5 m is never taken; where a hump or a gully stands in a block's place the block is left as woods (`plan.woods`, with its reason, the land's or the budget's) and the grid goes round it, and two parts either side of the cove are possible where that is where the land is. The budget of blocks is held as well to the town's houses (`growth.ts`, `lotBudget`; every number of the change is in `port/sizing.ts`, authored): growth stops when the lots the blocks taken would hold (their area over 520 m2 a lot) reach 330 and, for each block the history's budget stands over 26, 3 more, so that a town's lots come to about the buildings it holds (the game's town: 28 blocks, 317 lots, 291 buildings) and the grid does not run on over land the houses do not reach. The business street is short: a town the panic of 1893 stopped has one block of shops either side of the main street (the block on one side of the wharf street, its dock side when the founding year is even), a town that kept its boom one more in the row nearer the main street, where the land lets them; the dock district is the one block of the quay's row a block or two past the wharf street (it was every such block); the highway strip takes 30 percent of the blocks beside the highway (it was 65); a tavern is a few in a hundred shop units (the counted towns' 3.7 percent). The blocks the business street no longer takes are houses, so the game's town holds about 210 houses to 42 shop units; the town's fill raises every district's chance of a building until 280 stand or 88 percent of the lots do, and the cap past which houses thin out is 340 to 420. Streets are the grid's lines pruned to the blocks that exist (`port/ends.ts`) and then, once the buildings stand, cut back to what the buildings serve (`port/trim.ts`: from each free end inward to the first stretch with a building within 20 m of it or the first junction, a street that serves nothing dropped where the network stays whole, an alley dropped where under 55 percent of it has a building near; the stretches cut away are the unbuilt plat, whose lots stay and whose streets are gone), so that about 10 percent of the town's streets have no building near, and where a street would climb more than 15 percent over 16 m on the coast's own ground it is cut there and the stretch is a public STAIR (`port/stairs.ts`, a `stair` street of 3.5 m of steps in the street's right of way; 0 to 9 a town over forty worlds). Where the town's blocks end the town thins out (`port/outskirts.ts`): single buildings on lots of their own, each a block of one lot (`outskirts` district) and each with a reason (`plan.outskirts`): a gas station and a motel on the highway where the last blocks end, a tavern, a church, houses on large lots, a junkyard, a shake mill, a volunteer fire hall, fewer the further out (0.8 chance of one at the town's edge falling to none 560 m out), and a few along the first 240 m of a road out; each on land the plan can build on, off the railroad's corridor. The railroad's main line (`rail.ts`, 16 m seaward of the highway) is kept clear (no block, lot or building within 5 m of it); streets cross it at grade (`plan.rail.crossings`), and the town's own track is a SPUR (`plan.rail.spurs`, `port/spur.ts`): where the history has a railroad it leaves the main line beyond the end of the town on the mill's side, curves to the strip between the water and the blocks, runs past the mill and along the quay and on to the terminal, every bend an arc of 70 m radius or more. The depot stands in the block beside the main line nearest the core. The blocks keep clear of the main line's bank as well as its corridor (`railBank.ts`: the bank's width grows with how far the highway's grade line stands over the made ground at the line, so blocks stand 24 to 50 m from the highway's centre), a street that would end in the corridor is run on across it to the highway (a recorded crossing) or cut back to its edge, and no outlying lot stands on the sea's side of the highway, where it would have to cross the line to be reached. Where the main line stands higher than 10 m at both ends of the town (the highway climbs a headland there) the town has no spur. The spur's corners are rounded with arcs of 85 m and, where the legs are too short, relaxed until none bends tighter than 65 m; its points are 8 m apart.

**What stands beside each street, and how each lot is kept** (`port/edges.ts`, `port/yards.ts`; `plan.edges` by street id, `plan.yards` by lot id; each on a stream of its own). The history decides, as it did in the towns of this coast, and every number is authored (the research found none). A street's ERA is `core` (the main street, the avenue, streets between shops and the civic blocks: a sidewalk on both sides all the way, 3.5 to 4 m wide, kept up), `old` (the old plat's house streets: 88 percent of sides have any, a gap of 5 to 25 m about every 100 m, worn), `later` (the company's plat: none 35 percent, one side 45, both 20; a sidewalk may stop mid-block where the money ran out) or `edge` (the later plat's last streets, over 400 m from the core: none 60 percent, one side 30, both 10). A sidewalk is poured in RUNS of 18 to 60 m, each its own decade (1900 to 2020, weighted to the booms and the 1950s) and wear (its age, the era and a vacant lot beside it), each with a joint spacing of 1.35 to 1.8 m. A side has a CURB with its sidewalk in the core and the old plat, mostly in the later plat and rarely on the edges; the curb stands 15 cm over the gutter where the street was rebuilt and each overlay of asphalt buried 3.5 cm of it, to 5 cm: the roadway has one to four stretches of PAVING, each with a decade, a curb height and a wear, so the curb steps down where the street was overlaid. A side with no curb has a gravel shoulder 0.9 to 1.6 m wide and, on more than half of the later and edge streets, a shallow ditch. A SHOP'S DOOR OPENS ONTO A SIDEWALK whatever the era: after every street's own draws, every stretch of a grid street's side that a business or civic front stands on (storefront, tavern, restaurant, office, bank, hotel, lodge, depot, church, school, hospital, motel, garage and the rest with a door on the street) is given a sidewalk and a curb for its frontage and on to the nearest junction or neighbouring run (60 m at most), poured in its own decade (`addShopWalks`; a test over 40 seeds asks every unit door of `plan.uses` for a sidewalk run at its station). Over the 12 worlds of the pass (and 40 seeds in `test/town/edges.test.ts`): sidewalk on 100 percent of core street sides, 92 percent of the old plat's, 48 percent of the later plat's and 24 percent of the edges'. A lot's YARD is `mown`, `shaggy`, `tall`, `bare`, `mossy`, `brush` (or `paved`, in a paved district) from its building's state and era, the district and a roll of its own: a vacant lot is tall grass, brush or shaggy, a ruin brush, a vacant building shaggy or tall or mossy, the lots of the outskirts barer; a house lot has a drive about half the time (gravel, dirt two-track, or a concrete ribbon) and a front walk six times in ten as broken as the yard is neglected.

How the history decides the form:

| In the history | In the plan |
|---|---|
| What the town was founded for (sawmill, cannery, customs act, railroad bet) and when | The town's name, the founder's, and with a customs act a customs house among the landmarks |
| What the first plat was laid to: the wagon road that became the highway, or (where the highway runs near north) the line of the first wharf or a railroad's survey | The old plat's bearing: the highway's own line, or one 22 to 37 degrees off the compass; so the highway is the old main street, or cuts across the old blocks |
| The second plat, a land company's or a railroad's, laid to the compass | The compass plat wrapped around the old one; where the two bearings differ by 25 degrees or more, a seam where the grids do not line up |
| A railroad, and when | A spur from the main line along the flats' shore and the quay, between the water and the town, and a depot beside the main line; none without a railroad |
| A fire, and whether it took the waterfront too | A core of the later era and material (brick, or concrete after 1921), the main street widened; without a fire an older core, some of it in wood |
| The 1893 bust | More vacant buildings, and a boom-era hotel never filled |
| The county seat | A courthouse on the hill if the town is the seat, and none otherwise |
| The public port after 1911, what it handles | A terminal on the flats |
| The mill: which side, when it opened, closed or working, cleaned up or not | The mill's site on the north or south flats; a closed mill's site has no open mill building, a cleared one fewer buildings than a fenced one |
| The present-day layer | The core's vacant share, a tsunami refuge on the hill or not, a strip of motels and big stores along the highway or not |

The streets are the two plats' grids, the wharf street, the rail and alleys where a block has them. Rights of way are those of a small
Northwest town (`ways.ts`): an ordinary street 14 to 16 m with a roadway of 7.5 to 9 m, sidewalks of 1.7 m and a grass verge between
sidewalk and curb (a sidewalk in its place between shops and on the working waterfront); the main street 18 m with 11 m of roadway and
sidewalks of 3.5 m (20, 12 and 4 after a fire); the one avenue 20 m with 12 m of roadway and a painted median; alleys 4 m of gravel with no
sidewalk; nothing is wider than 12 m of roadway beside the highway's 11 m bed. The blocks keep their centre-line sizes, so lots gain the
difference. A grid street runs only as far as the last block it serves (`port/ends.ts` cuts every stub past its last intersection or fronting
lot, with 4 m past the last lot); each end that is no junction is named in `plan.ends` as a landing at the water or a made end, a turnaround or a
barricade. A few roads leave town (`plan.roads`, `port/roads.ts`): from the grid's inland ends a county road to the cemetery on the hill (every
town) and on most an old logging road to a landing, a gravel pit or a water tank, and, where a street on the flats side reaches the grid's end and
there is room, a road along the shore's flats to a landing, a gate or a pit (none of the forty tested seeds has one). Each road is stepped across the
site's ground grid in 12 m steps, the heading turning by exact Pythagorean rotations, costed by the grade along and across it, held to 9 percent a step
and 7 percent over the last 24 m (loosened only where no road is found), and narrows from paved to gravel to two wheel tracks; its destination is a
landmark of no building (`Landmark.at`) and a sentence of the history. Blocks are cut by the shore and the highway; a district's lot rule
(`port/rules.ts`) cuts its lots, with real widths (commercial lots 7.5 to 15 m, house lots 15 m); each lot is built on or left empty, with a kind, an era, a material, a roof and a state (open, vacant or
ruin) that follow from its district and the history. Landmarks reserve their blocks before the lots are cut and are bound to
the buildings that take them afterwards: the FBI office (a leased floor of a federal building or an office block, one block
off the main street), the hotel, the bank, a lodge hall, a depot, the port's terminal, two churches, the high school, the
hospital, and as the history says a customs house, a courthouse and a tsunami refuge. Grids are turned to bearings that are
Pythagorean triples (`bearings.ts`), so a plat's rotation is exact arithmetic.

**Junctions** (`junctions.ts`, `junctionShape.ts`; `plan.junctions`; its figures are the published research "How a small-town street intersection is built", the section named beside each in the code's comments). A junction is found where two streets of the grid meet (their centre lines cross, or one ends within the other's right of way) or a street ends within the highway's bed (the bed's edge, `ROAD_BED_HALF`, is a leg of its own with no pavement of ours); meetings within 3.5 m are one junction. Each has its LEGS, counter-clockwise, each a ray with the street's roadway, walk and width (`mouth`: where the leg's roadway begins; `bandLeft`, `bandRight`: where its curb, verge and sidewalk begin on each side), and its CORNERS, each a curb return (`radius`, tangent points `ta` and `tb`, centre `c`, the point `v` where the two curbs meet, and `lot` where the two lot lines meet) or a straight wedge (a tee's through side) or a sharp corner. Its KIND is a four-way (or a compound of more legs), a bend (two streets that end on each other's ends), a tee (with its stem), a skew (a four-way crossing more than 15 degrees off square: `skew` is the sine of that angle), a highway junction, or one of four that share no pavement: an alley's mouth (a driveway apron), a road end (a street's end where a road out goes on, with where the pavement ends), a stair, a rail crossing (crossbucks and stop line from the line's centre). Radii are 10 ft (3.0 m) between neighbourhood streets, 20 ft (6.1 m) with the main street or the avenue, 30 ft (9.1 m) to the highway's edge; authored: an obtuse corner keeps a square one's tangent and takes the radius that gives it, an acute one's tangent stops at 10 m, and two junctions so close that their returns would meet are `tight` (every corner 3.0 m), and where that is not enough they are one compound junction. CONTROL: none between quiet residential streets crossing within 15 degrees of square, STOP on a tee's stem, on the lesser street of a skewed crossing or of a marked one, and on every street meeting the avenue, the main street or the highway, and all-way stops at the one or two busiest corners of the core; there are no signals. Marked crosswalks are on the main street's and the avenue's legs, at the school's and the hospital's corners, and across the highway at the junctions of the core; every other crossing is unmarked. Every rebuilt corner has curb ramps (two per corner of a four-way) where both sides have a curb and a sidewalk; a corner has its curb return and its corner sidewalk only where both legs it joins have them on the sides that meet (`JunctionCorner.curb`, `.walk`, from `plan.edges`), and otherwise the pavement turns in a plain radius of asphalt into gravel, with no ramp; authored: 60 percent of the old plat's and 30 percent of the land company's residential junctions have older ramp-less corners. Where two streets cross within 60 degrees (the seam's sharp crossings) the lesser street's nearer leg is closed with a planter and a sign, and where two lie nearly one along the other (within 26 degrees) the lesser is held back until its roadway clears the other's, each leg closed with a planter (`closed`, `legacy: "planter"`); no island is made. The drawing and the pavement's shape read these records; a junction's polygon is `pavementPolygon`.

**What is in the buildings** (`port/business/`; `plan.uses`, one `BuildingUse` for each building, by id; the stage runs last, from the finished plan and the `business` stream, each building drawing from a stream of its own, so the streets, blocks, buildings and junctions of a seed are byte for byte what generator 6 made: `determinism.test.ts` holds three digests of the plan without `version` and `uses`). **NOTHING OF IT IS DRAWN YET**; it is data for a later drawing pass. A building's front is a `FrontFrame` (the left end of the front wall as seen from the street, the outward normal, the way along it) and all positions are `s` along it and `d` into the building. A shop, office, tavern, hotel, lodge hall, bank, gas station, motel, garage or big-box store has UNITS (bays) that tile its front exactly, cut at widths drawn from its own stream (a 15 m lot may be a 5 m barber and a 10 m shop; a strip of shops under one roof 3 to 6 bays), each with a door where its plan puts it (an old one recessed, centred, a third in or at the corner; a postwar one flush at an end; never forced to the middle), its windows between piers of differing widths, the height of its fascia, and, for about half the buildings of before 1960, one `remodel` (a widened opening, a bricked one, a dropped fascia, a new door). A building of two storeys or more has a narrow street door to its stairs inside one unit's frontage, and its upper floors have a use (apartments, offices, a lodge hall, storage, hotel rooms, empty) and windows. Each unit has a STATE (open; closed for the day; vacant for lease, with one window papered; vacant and boarded), a TENANT (trade, name, the decade it opened, never before the building's era or the trade's invention) and the earlier tenants (`past`, a building of 1905 has had several), the year it was vacated, and whether its lights are on. TRADES (`trades.ts`) are 73 (65 open today, the rest of the past only) with the mixes counted from the cached OpenStreetMap extracts of 21 coastal towns (the weights are the counts; the district multipliers, widths and earliest decades are authored); the history asks for marine trades only in a fishing port, a union hall in a mill town, lawyers and a title company at a county seat, kites and taffy on the tourist road; every town has a grocery, a cafe, a hardware store and one post office. NAMES (`names.ts`, authored placeholders) are an owner's surname or given name and the trade, the town's or a street's name and the trade, a place word, initials, or the bare trade, unique in a town (the past tenants' too), some of them the families a street or a landmark is named for. SIGNS (`signs.ts`) are data: a kind (fascia, blade, glass lettering, wall painting, pole, sandwich board, banner, OPEN, FOR LEASE, ghost, board), its lines, the style of its decade (hand-painted, neon and porcelain, backlit box, vinyl), its condition 0 to 1, whether it is lit, and where it goes (wall, how far along, height, which side of the door); an earlier tenant's fascia is sometimes left up, so that its words no longer match; the civic and industrial buildings carry a church's name and service times, the school's name and mascot, the federal building's plaque, a fire hall's district, the port's tenant board, a gas station's price pole (invented brands, prices of the present day) and a motel's VACANCY pole. ROOMS (`interiors.ts`) give each unit a depth, a ceiling, an interior type and 4 to 24 oriented boxes (shelf runs, counters, tables, booths, stool rows, cases, coolers, washers, desks, racks, crates, a ladder and paint cans in a vacant unit) with something in every window, the counter on the side of the door with most floor, nothing in the door's way, and lights on or off. The first four metres behind the glass (the band) are what the street sees, so the plan fills them: after the room's own layout it makes sure the service point (a till counter, checkout lanes, the reception counter, the bar) has a part in the band where the width allows, stands a tall thing against each side wall inside it, and then lays a second rank between 1.45 and 4 m in across the bare stretches (tables and chairs, a booth along a side wall, high tables, display cases, stands, bins, endcaps, racks, a bench and a couch, cabinets, a plant, a water cooler, a folding table, a workbench, by the kind of room and the trade) until a share the unit's own hash and record set is furnished: most rooms 0.86 to 0.98, about one in six (more in an old shop) deliberately thin (`interior.thin`). A 1.2 m way in stays clear from the door's zone to the back of the band, and a thing is skewed or pushed to a wall or a neighbour here and there. Only the hash is drawn from, so the rest of a room is what it was; `roomFront.test.ts` measures it. Houses, apartments and the floors above have, for each window, a treatment (curtained, blinds, lit, dark, boarded, broken) and a sill (plant, lamp), from the household's habit. The OSM counts, the lists and every authored number are in the comments of their files.

**What stands in the streets, yards and docks** (`port/props/`; `plan.props`, the last stage, on a stream of its own, each category on a sub-stream of it; every authored number is in `tuning.ts`). Each prop has a cause in what the plan holds, never a scatter. Keep-out boxes (every junction's pavement and the crosswalk and clearance beyond it, drive and alley mouths, street ends, the spawn, front walks, buildings) are laid first and everything else is placed against them. **Vehicles**: at the curb of the core and old streets, one side of a narrow street and both of the main street, crooked a few degrees and off the curb by a hand to half a metre, thick outside the open diner, tavern and grocery (a unit's trade draws cars, a vacant front draws none), on the shoulder where there is no curb; in a house's drive and yard by whether it is lived in and how the yard is kept (a project car on blocks, a dead one in the tall grass, a camper in a side yard, trucks in a bare lot); in the highway strip's painted stalls (the same rule `groundGeometry.ts` paints them by, held by a test); in business lots; and on the harbour's apron. **Boats on trailers** in yards (more near the harbour) and on the apron. **Poles** every 35 to 45 m down one side of each street (the side and spacing by its era), behind the curb or at the shoulder's edge, crossarms on the older streets, leaning ones, stubs, transformers, a streetlight at some junctions, and a service drop to each building that has power. **Fences** by household (picket on a few mown front yards, weathered board along a side and the back, post-and-wire on edge lots, chain-link round working yards), cut at drives and walks, with gates. **Carts** by the house or left at the curb, dumpsters and pallets behind businesses, **mailboxes** on posts where there is no sidewalk (ragged groups of two to four on the edge streets, a cluster box in later additions), **gear** in a fisherman's yard and on the piers and quay. A dedupe pass repaints a lot whose set equals a neighbour's. `plan.props.solids` holds the boxes a walker may not pass (`propSolid.ts`).

### The harbour

The made ground along the quay is one convex polygon for each stretch of the quay with water seaward (`plan.quay.wet`), with collinear quay-side edges and no overlap, so the bank along the quay is one smooth slope. `plan.decks` lists every deck over the water (the quay's apron along each wet stretch, piers, the platforms of sheds on piles, the wharf street, and the boat basin's floats and gangways) as an oriented rectangle with the height of its top (`DECK_TOP` 2.6 m, floats `FLOAT_TOP` 0.4 m, a gangway's from one to the other): the one list the walking and the drawing both read. `plan.harbour` (`port/harbour.ts`, its own random stream) holds the rest, decided by the history:

- **The basin and its boats.** One or two main floats parallel to the quay, 22 m out, each reached by a gangway from the apron, with finger floats and slips of three classes. A cannery town or a seafood port keeps a full basin of working boats; a mill, customs or railhead town fewer and more pleasure boats and sailboats; the share of the core's vacant storefronts is also the share of empty slips and the weight of derelicts (a few degrees' list, faded, gear gone) and of one that has sunk at its mooring. Boats (`{ id, kind, x, z, heading, length, beam, draft, state, colour, name, mooring }`; troller, gillnetter, crabber, tug, skiff, sailboat, pleasure; 4.5 to 20 m) lie in slips, alongside piers, a few at anchor, and a tug by the terminal's barge. They do not move.
- **Pilings.** Fields of dead piles (uneven tops, some leaning, gaps) where a closed mill's or a cannery's wharf was pulled down (or an old fish dock), with the year in a sentence of the history's list; dolphins (three piles lashed) off the longer pier ends.
- **The mill's water.** A working mill has a log pond held by a boom of sticks chained between piles, floating logs, and a jack ladder to a jack house; a closed mill's boom is empty and, if the mill is old enough and not cleared, the rusting wigwam burner stands on its site.
- **The terminal.** A yard by what the port handles (decks of logs in trapezoid stacks and perhaps a chip pile, silos and a bulk shed, rows of containers, rows of cars), on the terminal's open lots (the terminal's blocks are built to 42 per cent so there is room), and at the port's wharf (the wharf street's pier, in the cove's deep water) a loaded barge with its tug and, for grain and chips, a loading spout. A terminal the history says is idle (more likely the emptier the town) has an empty yard and no barge.
- **Lights.** A white harbour light on one headland's tip and a day beacon on the other (the tongues are the samples where the site's shore runs far seaward), and a light at the end of the longest pier. Structures only: no lamps are lit.

Depth is not read from the ground (the plan reads the site alone): it is estimated from the distance seaward of the shore, one way in the cove between its headlands and a shallower one outside, and checked in `test/town/harbour.test.ts` against the coast's real floor, which finds about 2 per cent of boat footprint points over a sea stack the site cannot see.

### The ground the game lays over the coast

What the game lays over the engine's coast is `CAPE_PLACE` (`place.ts`), a `CoastOverlay` that the coast is registered with
(`coast.ts`). It is made of:

- **The cover** (`cover.ts`). The made ground follows the plan, not a rectangle (an outlying lot's window is small, 6 m held and 30 m of fall, and levels nothing: its ground is the land's own, and only the cliff bands and the forest keep off it): its weight is the smooth union of a window over each block polygon (whole to 16 m beyond a block's edge, falling to nothing over the next 160 m) and a window about the quay's line, found through a 24 m grid so a terrain sample tests only the blocks near it. Beyond it the ground is the coast's own.
- **The made ground** (`ground.ts`). Where the cover holds, the ground is a graded plane over the coast's own, built as before: 2.5 m above the sea at the water's edge, rising at a grade set at each z so that its height at the highway is exactly the highway's grade line, easing to a hill of 4 percent inland of the highway that steepens to 8 percent between 200 and 420 m (where the coast's own hill does), with a bank at the water's edge. It keeps relief: a saturated share of the coast's own height over the plane (0.2 on the flats, 0.3 on the hill, at most 2 m either way: the coast's own humps under the town are tens of metres across and several high; raised from 0.12, 0.2 and 1.2 m) and low relief of its own from the engine's derivative-carrying noise (3 m of amplitude on the flats, 2 on the hill, 140 m wavelength). None of it stands on the fill, within 14 m of the quay, within 14 m of the highway or by the water's edge, each opening smoothly. Measured over the ten seeds the pass was measured on, before the town was laid over the land and after (relief 0.12, 0.2 and 1.2 m, then 0.2, 0.3 and 2 m): the rise across a block on the flats had a median of 0.37 m and a 90th percentile of 0.81 (greatest 1.55), and has 0.41, 2.08 (5.4); on the hill 3.72, 5.28 (7.9) and 3.81, 5.98 (10.6). Of 17000 windows of 20 m along streets none was over 12 percent, and now 20 of 14900 are (0.13 percent, on the ends of streets that meet the highway across a hillside; the greatest is 27 percent), and 6 and 4 of 3000 footprints span over 2.5 m. The relief cannot be raised further within what a person can build on: the plane's own 4 to 8 percent on the hill, and the land's up to 14, make a block rise 4 to 6 m whatever the share, and each raise of the share puts more footprints over 2.5 m (a share of 0.8 and 0.9 puts 59 of 2850 there). What keeps the made ground near the land is that a block is taken only where the land is within 4.5 m of the plane: the ground is moved 1.6 m at the median in a block and 19 m at the worst. Its height and slope are exact derivatives, as the engine's passes are.
- **The fill behind the quay** (`fill.ts`). Where the plan's straight quay lies seaward of the natural shore, one convex polygon of fill for each wet stretch of the quay (`plan.fill`, `plan.quay.wet`) raises the ground to the quay's height over what was sea, with a 6 m bank at the quay's edge: flat, at the quay's height (`levels.ts`, shared with the harbour's decks and apron, which stand a step above it), and exactly as the harbour was built to; the relief window is shut on it and within 14 m of the quay's line, so the apron and every pier's landward end meet the ground. The plan reads the coast's own ground and the made ground reads the plan's fill, so nothing loops. The shore between the site's samples is a monotone cubic spline (`shoreSpline.ts`), so the gradient stays exact.
- **The climb to the massif drawn out** (`blend.ts`). On the coast's own the climb ends 40 to 500 m inland of the shore, so the ground behind the highway would rise tens of metres in the town's first hundred and sixty. Inside a window along the shore the climb is drawn out to the world's own end (the engine's `blend` hook, whose end and window are handed the seed): the end is drawn from 800 to 2400 m inland (the mean of two draws, so the middle is likeliest), the window's half length from 450 to 1100 m of coast and its centre up to 600 m north or south of the cove (the same), so one world has a broad low plain behind the cove, another a narrow shelf with the mountain close, and the hill inland of the highway is a slope and not a wall. Buildable land within the town's reach is 24 to 61 ha over forty worlds. The highway does not move; its grade line, which follows the ground, comes down with the foot of the hill.
- **The forest by the lot** (`clearing.ts`). The engine asks the place for a feature mask at a tree cell's centre or a ground-cover sample: shut over every street's right of way and 1.5 m of it, every building's footprint and 3.5 m of it (more, by the engine's tree jitter of up to 5 m, since a tree stands off its cell's centre), every paved block (the core, the dock end, the working waterfront, the mill's site, industrial flats, the highway strip) and the cleared places about a road's destination; open on yards (house lots, the churchyard, the school's and hospital's grounds, vacant lots) at 22 percent of the forest's trees and whole for ground cover; whole beyond the outermost blocks, up to the back fences; and no loose rock or driftwood on the town. The engine paints the ground by its height (sand and pebble under 4 m) and gates grass on 7 m and trees on 4 m, and its mask's `meadow` only thickens the grass and `bare` is not read, so a yard on the made flats (2 to 3 m) would paint as the beach's pebble; the place's `shoreStrip` hook (`townStripWeight`) says a yard's ground reads 12 m higher to the shore's rules, so a yard paints as grass and bears trees and grass, and `meadow` thickens its lawn. The strip is also every street's right of way and 2 m past it (`STRIP_RAMP`) and every paved block, so the terrain under and beside the pavement paints as soil and a sliver that shows at an edge is dirt at the edge of the asphalt, never a stripe of beach (before, 40 to 70 percent of the terrain under the town's flat surfaces painted as sand). The sand no longer keeps trees and grass off a block's edge, so a paved block shuts both beyond its edge by their jitter (`blockWindowOff`). A road clears the forest only to its own width and a ditch, so the trees close over it. The mask reads the street and the lot (the engine's blades are 0.5 m cells, so it can say a verge from a sidewalk): shut within a metre of a roadway's edge (the curb and the trough), shut on a sidewalk's slabs but for the back 45 cm (30 percent), 12 percent on a gravel shoulder, on a verge the grass of the lot beside it (70 percent of its ground cover, ramped in over 0.8 m), in an alley shut between the wheel tracks' lines and open beside and between them; and a yard is its lot's own (`YARD_MASK`: how thick and tall the grass, `meadow`, and the share of trees: mown 0.4 cover, shaggy 1.1, tall 2.1 with meadow 1, bare 0.1, mossy 0.7 under 0.32 of the trees, brush 2.4), with a drive's or a walk's rectangle shut. The cliff bands are kept off where the town's cover holds, and along the roads out (`townKeepD`). Round a building whose lot is a yard the mask is shut only inside its walls and 0.3 m beyond, so the engine's grass grows to the wall's foot; the place's `clutterStands` (`townClutterStands`) then keeps each clump, fern, bush and shrub its own half width off the wall by where it stands itself, and the ground before the front door (its steps or porch) stays bare. A building on pavement keeps its ring of 2 m.

- **The streets cut into it** (`streetField.ts`). A real street is graded so that its sidewalk and the lots beside it stand at one level and the roadway lies a curb's face below: so after the made ground the place's `shapeD` cuts it down along every roadway, by the curb's height there (the paving's `curb`, 15 cm at most, none on a side with no curb; the two sides' heights lie across the roadway as a fall), across the pavement of every junction (the deepest of its legs' curbs), the window 1 over the roadway to the curb line and 0 by 30 cm beyond it (a grade of 0.94 at the steepest for 15 cm: a person can stand on it), and a seam between two paving stretches blending over 1.2 m. A shallow ditch (22 cm, 1.8 m wide) lies beside a side with no curb and no sidewalk. The sidewalk, the verge and the lawns behind keep the made ground's height, so a sidewalk is level with its lot. The engine draws the terrain as a mesh of 1 m (2 m on the low tier) that cannot hold a step of 15 cm over a few, and it stood through the roadway: the terrain is therefore cut a further 20 cm under every roadway (fading over 1.4 m beyond it) and 12 cm under every sidewalk's pours (fading over 0.6 m beyond the lot line: a slab is a flat triangle of up to 4 m over ground that keeps its relief, and 5 cm let the terrain through on a bump), and what is walked and drawn is the terrain plus that fill (`streetLiftD`; `withStreets` is the walking field, wrapped under the decks and trestles in `sim.ts`, and `townGround.heightAt` the drawing's). The values and the slopes are analytic (a ditch where it meets another street's roadway leaves its window's slope out). The terrain is cut 12 cm out of sight under the town's other paved ground too (`HIDDEN_PAVED`, fading over 0.6 m beyond it: every polygon of `pavedGround.ts`, below). `TOWN_GROUND` is 9. The engine's clipmap is an upper envelope that grows coarser (and blends toward the next ring from the middle of each) with distance, so no cut holds the terrain under a street at 2 m spacing or more; there the surface is held by its depth step, which grows with the square of the distance, and what is measured is the rings of 2 m or finer (`test/townWorld/terrainUnderStreets.test.ts`, which builds the engine's real rings, `clipTerrain.ts`). What the engine's mesh stands over a surface at the lip of a drop to the sea (a street's end at the water, where the made ground falls 2 m in 4: the envelope lifts a 2 m mesh by up to a metre) is the engine's to change; the game cannot hold it down (and it does not show: the flat layer's depth offset is about 1.43e-4 times the distance squared in metres, 36 cm at 50 m, and over every ring and every lip the terrain beats it at fewer than 1 sample in 10,000 of the town's surfaces, which the test asserts). And what shows of the terrain at an edge paints as soil: see the strip below.

- **Where animals may not be** (`wildlifeGround.ts`). The engine's ambient wildlife (elk, deer, rabbits, squirrels, birds, butterflies) is placed from the world's seed by the terrain's own rules, and the town's lawns make the whole town forest ground to them, so herds would stand in the streets. The overlay's `wildlifeGround` hook (asked of a species and a place, only where the engine's own rules allow the animal) says no: elk and deer are kept out of the town, and rabbits to the house yards within 90 m of its edge. The town is read once per seed on a 16 m grid over the plan's bounds: a cell is the town if any part of it is within 25 m of a built block (not the outskirts' lone lots), a street's right of way, the quay, the fill behind it or the terminal's yard, and so is every cell the town wholly encloses (a wooded block among built ones). A cell's depth is its distance to the nearest cell that is not the town. A rabbit in the town is let stay only within 90 m of the edge and only where the nearest building (within 30 m) is a house. Squirrels and butterflies are not asked; birds are never asked. The hook is the renderer's alone: it is no tunable, moves no plan or ground, and is no part of the level id.

Every constant that shapes this ground is in the overlay's tunables, which the engine makes part of the level id.

### How the town is drawn

`client/src/game/town/` turns the plan and the ground's height into plain arrays (positions, normals, vertex colours,
indices), with no renderer, so the builders are tested as pure functions:

- **Streets and junctions** (`streetGeometry.ts`, `junctionGeometry.ts`, `junctionPaint.ts`, `signGeometry.ts`, `surface.ts`, `junctionWorld.ts`, `signFont.ts`). Every surface is the walked ground's height (which is already cut down for the roadway: see "The ground the game lays over the coast") plus a lift of a centimetre or so: a roadway 1.2 cm, the sidewalk's slabs 1.2 cm with up to 1.6 cm of heave at a joint (never more than 3 cm over the lot's ground), a curb's top at the lots' level (read 30 cm past the curb line) over a face down to the gutter, a corner sidewalk 2.8 cm; a vertex follows the ground's roll (a junction's cross-slopes are resolved by this: its pavement is draped over the ground as a street's strips are, with no plane of its own). A street is drawn by what its side has (`plan.edges`): the roadway in its paving stretches (the new dark asphalt after 2000 where under half worn, else the old, cracked one, each with a tone of its own, the texture running along the street, a seam across between stretches), the curb in sections of about 3 m (some replaced, lighter), the sidewalk slab by slab (`walkGeometry.ts`: tooled joints every 1.35 to 1.8 m by the pour, each pour's tone by its decade, slab by slab heave at the joints, cracks, broken corners, slabs gone and filled with asphalt or gravel, dirt and moss thickening toward the back edge and in the joints, weeds in the joints and over the edge, a back edge that wanders by up to 18 cm, a rusted cover or two), and where a side has no curb an asphalt edge that wanders by 10 to 20 cm against a gravel shoulder with its own ragged edge. `roadWear.ts` lays utility cuts (darker rectangles at angles, some overlapping, with sealed seams), sealed cracks, potholes on the least-used streets and the worst paving, the gutter's band of needles and dirt, gravel dragged out of alley mouths, manholes and storm drains; the paint is worn (some dashes short, some gone, the rest faded by the street's use). `yardGeometry.ts` lays each lot's drive (gravel bed, dirt two-track, concrete ribbon), front walk, bare and mossy patches, and the worn verge (a drive's apron, a desire line from the curb to the sidewalk, a pull-off for a bare yard). An alley is two wheel tracks of gravel with grass between. A street is cut where it meets another: its roadway strip ends at each junction's `mouth`, its verge, sidewalk and curb at the corner's tangent point, and the pavement between is the junction's own, ONE surface bounded by its curb returns (an ear-cut polygon split in four, once or twice, so no triangle is much over 9 m), drawn first; the strips of roadway laid after it are laid without any ground already laid (`RoadSet`: a convex tile minus the convex pieces it overlaps), so no two roadway surfaces overlap anywhere. The corner is a curb along the return and the sidewalk behind it in one piece to the two lot lines; a ramp is cut through the curb at each side of each leg's crosswalk (where both sides have a curb and a sidewalk), 4 ft wide, laid in strips across so it follows the ground's own cut (the ramp's run is the ground's 30 cm, not the research's 1.81 m: the walking and the drawing agree, which a smooth ramp would not), with a 2 ft yellow dome panel at its foot; a ramp-less corner has a full-height curb round, and a corner where a leg has no curb is a plain asphalt radius with a gravel shoulder. The paint is the research's: ladder bars 2 ft wide with gaps of 5 ft at most, 10 ft long, just outside the pavement on each marked leg (across the highway they lie on the engine's road at its own lift, 13.5 cm); a stop line 18 in wide 4 ft before the crosswalk (6 ft from the pavement where none is marked); the main street's centre line in 10 ft dashes with 30 ft gaps ending 5 ft short of a crosswalk and carried through the junction as a dotted line of 2 ft dashes with 6 ft gaps; parking stalls held back 20 ft from a crosswalk and 30 ft from a STOP sign; the avenue's median ending short of the junction; none at all on a residential junction or its streets. Signs are geometry: a STOP sign (a red octagon 30 in across on a post 1 ft behind the curb, its bottom 7 ft over the curb's top, "STOP" in white), green street-name blades on a post at one corner (a blade for each street, parallel to it, the plan's name in capitals, as geometry from a 5 by 7 pixel face, `signFont.ts`, on both faces on the full tier and blank on the low), a DEAD END sign, a PAVEMENT ENDS diamond, three red diamond markers 18 in on a side 4 ft over the road at a turnaround. A T's through street runs unbroken across its top; an alley's mouth is a flared driveway apron through the verge with the curb a lip, the sidewalk carried straight across at its own level and the alley's gravel rising to it. The wharf is a timber deck; ground under the sea is not drawn; a tile that reaches into the engine's road bed is cut along its edge (paint across the highway is not).
- **Made ends** (`endsGeometry.ts`): the pavement stops at a clean line and gravel runs beyond for 6 m, a bulb for a turnaround, a barricade of two striped boards on three posts, a timber bumper at a landing.
- **Where a house meets the ground** (`houseSeam.ts`, `seamWall.ts`, `seamGround.ts`, and the growth at the walls in `growth.ts`). The house builder notes each house's walls, foundation, door and steps or porch; the passes after it dress the seam by the household's record (the yard's upkeep, the house's era and state): a foundation in dull concrete, block or brick with splash, algae and uneven skirt boards; ragged patches of soil, moss, gravel or bark along the walls, wider at a downspout; things at the door and against the walls; patches of clover, moss, straw and bare earth on the lawn; ferns, dock and bramble at the walls of a house nobody keeps. The grass itself is the engine's, let up to the wall by the clearing.
- **Paved ground** (`groundGeometry.ts`): on each block of a paved district a patch 4.8 cm over the ground in 8 m tiles (a concrete walk in the core, gravel on the dock end, the mill's site and industrial flats, asphalt on the waterfront and the highway strip, which has a row of painted stalls where a whole stall fits clear of the buildings). The paved polygons are one list the simulation side owns (`sim/town/pavedGround.ts`, cached per plan): those blocks, and the APRON of each gas station, motel and fire hall that stands alone beyond the last blocks (the lot inset 2.5 m at its sides and back and 0.5 m at the street, and no deeper than 3 m behind the building: asphalt, with the unpaved margin left to weeds). The drawing lays its patches by that list, and the clearing (`clearing.ts`) shuts trees and every kind of ground cover over each polygon and a margin beyond it (a block's `BLOCK_MARGIN`, an apron's `APRON_MARGIN`), and the streets' field (`streetField.ts`) cuts the terrain under it, so the three cannot disagree about where there is paving. The lot's drive, walk and patches are not laid on an apron.
- **Roads out** (`roadGeometry.ts`): strips by surface (asphalt, gravel, two wheel tracks) narrowing along the road, and each destination drawn crudely: a cemetery's fence and headstones, a landing's logs, a pit, a tank on legs, a gate.
- **Surfaces** (`townSurface.ts`, `groundSurfaces.ts`, `buildingSurfaces.ts`): see "The town's textures", below. Streets and ground wear asphalt, sidewalk concrete and gravel; buildings their walls' and roofs' layers.
- **Buildings** (`buildingGeometry.ts` and the files it calls; see "Buildings from the plan", below): shells on the plan's oriented rectangles (on sloping ground the floor is the highest ground under the footprint and the walls run down to the lowest; a front on a slope is entered at the sidewalk's height), roofs by kind (flat with a parapet, gable, hip, shed; the oldest wooden gables sag at the ridge), walls cut around their openings, fronts, rooms, windows, signs and parts from the plan's `uses`, vacant buildings dull and boarded, ruins roofless and broken at the top, and one touch for each landmark (a flagpole and portico for the FBI office, a steeple, an open tower on legs for the refuge, a mill's stack). Colour comes from the material and the building's id; no random call is made.
- **The quay** (`quayGeometry.ts`): along each wet stretch an apron (a deck) and a straight wall a hand's width beyond its edge, so the bank is hidden: timber cribwork under a timber cap, or, where a fire after 1921 took the waterfront, steel sheet piling under a concrete cap (`plan.quay.wall`), with wales or a corrugated face, fender piles every 6 m, ladders every 40 m and bollards every 15 m.
- **Decks and rails** (`pileGeometry.ts`): each deck of `plan.decks` with its top at exactly the height the walking uses, a skirt, and piles for piers and sheds; a timber bull rail with posts along every edge the simulation holds a walker back from.
- **Parked vehicles as models** (full tier; `tools/models/build.py`, `client/assets/models/`, `game/town/props/vehicle*.ts`). Twelve files (`vehicle.<id>.glb`, 11 MB in all) and a manifest (`vehicles.json`: footprint, wheels' contact points, roles) are written from the downloaded sources: each baked to one frame (metres, +x the front, on y = 0 at the tyres), joined into one mesh a role (`paint`, `glass`, `lamp`, `plate`, `tyre`, `under`, `interior`, or `body` for the worn ones), the paint a colour with no texture. The plan gives each vehicle its model (`sim/town/port/props/vehicleModels.ts`, from its kind, state, load, age and seed; a camper has none) and its box is the model's footprint, so the walker's box is the file's own; the room planned for a kind in `tuning.ts` is the largest of its models. `vehicleFleet.ts` stands each on the plane fitted through its four wheels' ground contacts, `vehicleLook.ts` gives it a paint from a table of dull period colours, grime, dust and (for derelicts and project cars) algae, `vehicleMaterial.ts` wears that by instance, and `vehicleParts.ts` loads the files once the town's geometry is made, one patient job a model, and draws a model's roles as thin instances culled each frame to the view and 160 m. The contact patch under each stays in the town's flat layer; a model that fails to load is left out. The low tier, campers and trailers stay code-built.
- **Props** (`game/town/props/`, drawn after the harbour, as a generator of slices): vehicles are lofted through stations from the rear bumper to the nose (a smooth-shaded `MeshBuilder.grid`; the wheel arches cut by raising the lower edge of the stations over each wheel; side glass and screens as the town's see-through effect over a dark cabin with seats you can make out; wheels in the arches; bumpers, lamps, mirrors, door seams), in the roof-bitumen layer (the smoothest of the town's layers) tinted by the paint, with rust at the arches and sills of old ones, grime low on the body, a faded roof and bonnet, and algae at the seals of one that has not moved; a work vehicle carries its gear, a project car stands on blocks under a tarp, a derelict sits low on flat tyres. Boats are lofted hulls on trailers (aluminium skiff in the sheet-metal layer, fibreglass runabout), some under a tarp, some with weeds through the trailer. Poles are tapered timbers of the weathered-boards layer; wires are three-sided tubes wider than a real wire, hung in a parabola. Fences follow the ground post by post; chain-link is a faint see-through sheet with rails and posts, because a mesh of wires would shimmer. Each vehicle has a dark patch of ground under it (the scene has no shadow). The low tier lofts through the shape's own stations, draws three vehicles in five with no interior, mirrors or seams, thinner poles and wires, no service drops, and fewer fences and gear.
- **Boats and the harbour's things** (`boatGeometry.ts`, `harbourGeometry.ts`): hulls lofted through six stations with a sheer line, wheelhouses with dark windows and the kind's gear; piles, boom sticks and logs, the jack ladder, the burner, the yard's stacks, silos, containers and cars, the barge, and the lights.
- **Batches** (`townGeometry.ts`): by cells of 128 m, one set of arrays for what lies flat and one for what stands, built as a generator of slices the engine's scheduler spreads over frames, so no frame stalls on the town.

`townParts.ts` is the renderer part: a `RenderGame` that wraps the engine's `noParts` and, once the scene is registered, makes
a Lite mesh of each batch as a job of the scheduler, the cells nearest the start first, under the town's material (`townMaterial.ts`), the engine's air and wet look as
its props are, with the flat layer drawn a depth step ahead of the terrain (toward the eye: the renderer's depth runs from 1 at the near plane to 0 far away, so the layer's steps and a rank's share are added to the clip depth, and `test/townWorld/depthLead.test.ts` holds their signs; with the other sign a surface with no cut under it, a yard's drive or walk, is covered by the terrain from a few metres off, and a mark by the surface it lies on. The layer takes no share of the engine's slope-scaled offset: that is worked out a vertex at a time, so two surfaces at one place got different shares wherever their triangles differ, by far more than a rank's step, and a patch was eaten by the roadway under it in a wedge that moved with the eye. A surface over ground the terrain is not cut under, a shoulder's outer edge or an alley's wheel tracks, lies `OPEN_GROUND_LIFT` (4 cm) over it, as a yard's surfaces do, since the terrain is drawn a few centimetres over the ground wherever it bends). Lite culls no mesh, so each frame shows a cell's
meshes while its bounding sphere is in the view's planes. The standing meshes are registered as hard cover for the rain (on
the tiers above low). The low tier builds simpler rooms, windows and trim (see "Buildings from the plan"); the
geometry is otherwise the same on all three tiers. The glass and the signs are meshes of their own, drawn blended after the opaque ones. One thing to know of Lite: its PBR pipelines are built when the scene is
registered, and know vertex colours only if a mesh of the scene had them then, so `townParts` stands one hidden triangle of no
size with a colour a vertex in the scene from the start (`early`), as the engine's lake panorama does. The town's textured materials need nothing of the seed: their pipelines (the UV, the plugin's vertex data and its two texture arrays) are made when the first mesh of one joins the registered scene, as the engine's props' plugin pipelines are.

### The town's textures

The town's geometry wears scanned CC0 PBR textures (each work credited in `CREDITS.md`) on its walls, roofs and street surfaces. Everything else is still flat vertex colour.

- **The files.** `tools/textures/build.py` turns the sources into `client/assets/textures/` (Git LFS): for each of 22 layers, `town.<name>.color.webp` (512 x 512, sRGB; a layer that is *paintable* (painted lap siding, stucco, aged corrugated metal and the generated asphalt shingles) is neutral grey detail, luminance over its mean times 0.5, for a paint colour to multiply) and `town.<name>.data.webp` (512 x 512 lossless RGBA, data: R, G the normal's x and y in the OpenGL convention, B roughness, A = 0.5 + 0.5 AO; AO is in the upper half of alpha because a browser that decodes through a canvas premultiplies). `town.layers.json` lists each layer: name, index, asset id, credit id (the work's section in `CREDITS.md`, which alone says where it came from), tile size in metres across and up (the figures the source gives, and the project's estimates, marked `tileEstimated`), paintable, generated. The layers are walls (siding_painted, planks_peeling, shingle_siding, planks_weathered, brick, metal_aged, metal_painted, concrete_board, stucco, stone, plywood, shutter), roofs (roof_metal, roof_bitumen, roof_shingle) and ground (sidewalk, asphalt, asphalt_dark, gravel, mud, moss, rust). `asphalt_dark` (new paving, a cut, a seam) and `mud` (grime: dirt in joints and gutters, worn ground) are used by the worn ground and the buildings; `moss` and `rust` are the wear overlays. The game registers its files beside the engine's (`assets.ts`, `mergeAssets`), by their names under `textures/`.
- **The builders' interface.** `MeshBuilder.poly(points, hint, colour, surface?)` (also `quad` and `wall`): a `Surface` is a layer name, a `UvFrame` in metres (an origin and two axes on the polygon's plane; each vertex's UV is its offset along each axis divided by the layer's tile size, so a texture is at true scale; swapping the axes turns it on its side), an optional `tint` (what the texture is multiplied by: by default the polygon's colour for a paintable layer and white for the rest) and a `wear` (a share 0 to 1 of the surface, a number or a function of a vertex's position) with a `wearKind` (`moss`, the default, `rust`, `peel`, `algae` or `grime`), whether it runs in `streaks`, and a second wear (`wear2`, `wearKind2`, `streaks2`) laid over the first. `colour` stays the flat colour the polygon is drawn in where there is no texture and until the textures land. `wallFrame`, `edgeFrame` and `groundFrame` make the usual frames. The arrays gain `uvs` and `surface` (twelve floats a vertex: layer or -1, the first wear's amount, its kind, the glow; tint rgb, how clear it is; the second wear's amount and kind, two spare). A polygon with no surface is unchanged.
- **The material** (`townMaterial.ts`, a Lite `MaterialPlugin`, priority 200). Per fragment, at `CUSTOM_FRAGMENT_UPDATE_DIFFUSE`: it samples the layer's colour and data arrays by the UV, multiplies the colour by the tint, sets roughness, occlusion and the normal (a tangent frame from screen-space derivatives of the world position and the UV: no tangent attribute), and lays two wears' overlays (moss, rust, peeling boards, algae film, grime: each kind's layer and colour grade in the plugin) where three octaves of world-space value noise are under the wear's amount, with a soft edge: patches, or, for streaks, noise stretched down the surface. Bare metal sheet is half metallic and the wear takes that away. A surface can also light itself (`glow`) and be see-through (`clear`, with the reflection by direction and the grime described under windows and glass), which is how a room's walls (baked light in the vertex tint), a lit sign and the glass work; the sign layer's plugin also reads the lettering atlas. A vertex with no layer is untouched. The vertex packing and the injection points are in the file's header. The colour array is an sRGB texture (filtering in linear light), the data array is data; both have mips and repeat. Lite takes no anisotropy.
- **Loading** (`townTextures.ts`). The arrays are made empty with the renderer's part and filled in place by one upload when the 44 images have been decoded (four at a time, the engine's decoder, `decodeGroundLayer`); until then the plugin's `townReady` uniform is 0 and the town draws as flat colour. The part's `waiting()` counts the images. If the arrays cannot be made the town stays flat colour, with one warning. On the low tier the arrays are 256 x 256 (a quarter of the memory), the wear has two octaves and its overlay brings colour and roughness but no second normal. The full tiers' arrays are about 62 MB of GPU memory with their mips, the low tier's 15 MB.
- **What is textured.** Walls by the plan's material (`buildingSurfaces.ts`: wood is painted lap siding in the house's colour, one in four in shingle siding, sheds and the sheds on piles half in weathered boards, some vacant houses in peeling boards; brick, concrete, stone, stucco and metal to their layers), with the courses running along the wall from the building's base and the four walls end to end round the building; roofs by kind (pitched: shingles with their courses along the eave; shed and metal: metal roofing with its ribs down the slope; flat: bitumen); boarded openings in plywood and big doors in the shutter layer; roadways and junction pavements in asphalt, sidewalks, curbs, ramps and the blocks' concrete walks in the sidewalk concrete, alleys and gravel yards and road ends in gravel, by the flat colour they are laid in (`groundSurfaces.ts`), with the world's x and z as their UVs. Wear is a share of the surface: a little on every wall, more on vacant buildings and ruins, more at a wall's foot; on the ground, dirt (the `grime` kind, in the second wear slot, over moss in the first) and moss by cause (see "How the town is drawn": sidewalks, roadways and yards).
- **Short-lived memory.** The terrain's functions used to answer with small objects (a value and its slopes), and every reading of the ground made hundreds: a load made about 13 GB of them in its first half minute, and a page at rest about 50 MB a second, which is what swells the heap between collections. They now leave their answers in registers: one record with fixed fields that lives as long as its module and is written in place, read into locals at once after the call (anything may write it again: the same helper is called many times, and the engine's coast and the town's ground ask each other). A register is a record and never a module's `let`, because a `let` holding a double is boxed again at each write. The engine's noise, coast, massif and rock layer do this from v0.2.13, and the town's ground (`sim/town/`: `fill.ts`, `cover.ts`, `streetField.ts`, `ground.ts`, `shoreSpline.ts`, `clearing.ts`, `railGround.ts`, and `place.ts`'s `shapeD`, which makes the one record the engine is owed) with it; exported functions keep their signatures as wrappers. A load makes about 4.9 GB where it made 11.5, and a page at rest about 9 MB a second. The numbers are the same to the bit: `test/townGround/groundDigest.test.ts` and `coverDigest.test.ts` hold digests taken from the code before the change, and a digest that moves means arithmetic moved. Earlier, the engine's wildlife director was given a lattice of kept heights for its lines of sight (v0.2.12). What is left is the numbers a call passes (a double handed to a function that is not inlined is boxed), the mesh builders' own points, and one record at each crossing between the engine and the place.
- **Model textures are KTX2.** The vehicles' images (`client/assets/models/`) and, from v0.2.10, the engine's own models' are KTX2, read through `KHR_texture_basisu` and transcoded to the GPU's block format at one byte a texel; as WebP, JPEG and PNG they decoded to four (about 470 MB of GPU memory between them, now about 120 MB). `tools/models/test/manifest.test.mjs` holds the vehicles to it. The town's own texture arrays and the sign atlas are still four bytes a texel.
- **A builder's vertices are 32-bit numbers from the start** (`meshBuilder.ts`, `Run`). A builder kept its positions, normals, colours, UVs, surface records and indices in plain arrays, eight bytes a number, and copied them into typed arrays at the end: while a town was built the tab's JS heap stood at 1.4 GB. Each is now a typed array that doubles as it fills, and `townGeometry` takes each cell's arrays out of its builder (`take`), which lets the builder's storage go. The heap's peak while a town loads is about 640 MB.
- **A mesh's arrays are let go once the GPU has them** (`leanMesh.ts`). Lite keeps what a mesh was made from and its plugin's per-vertex data for as long as the mesh lives, which held every vertex of the town twice (96 bytes in the tab and 96 on the GPU, about 200 MB of the tab's memory). The game reads none of it back, so `townParts.ts` and `railParts.ts` drop each mesh's arrays as soon as its buffers are written (the plugin's is written by the first frame that reaches the mesh).
- **Nothing is a flat colour: materials** (`materials.ts`). A polygon with no `Surface` of its own takes a material: a name (`"wood"`, `"boards"`, `"painted"`, `"steel"`, `"rusty"`, `"concrete"`, `"brick"`, `"cloth"`, `"plastic"`, `"rubber"` and the rest of the table) that stands for a layer and how it is laid. The frame is found from the polygon (level faces on the world's x and z, standing ones along themselves and up, a board's grain along its longest edge), and the builder's colour is kept: a paintable layer is painted it, any other layer is tinted to it over the layer's own mean colour. A builder names the material on the polygon (in the place of a surface), or round a block of code (`withMat`), or on a smooth grid (`GridLook.mat`). A polygon nobody named takes `"painted"` and is counted (`unnamed`); `client/test/townWorld/materials.test.ts` holds the count at zero and finds no opaque vertex without a layer in either tier. Only glass, wires and the sign atlas's faces carry no layer. The game has no layer for plastic, rubber, cloth, rope, galvanised sheet or smooth painted metal: those materials are stand-ins on the nearest layer (the fine grain of the sidewalk's concrete, the dark asphalt, the rust-and-paint layer), and are the first things a new layer would replace.
- **Doors** (`buildingKit.ts`: `doorIn`, `pairedDoors`, `glazed`). A door's leaf fills its hole from edge to edge and the hole's reveal runs exactly to the leaf, wherever the ledger put it, so nothing is seen round a door or past it. A house's or a hall's door carries its panel and its light on the leaf (glass over a curtain or the dark of the hall); a shop's is a kick panel under glass in a frame, with a transom or a rail to the head, so the room is seen through the glass alone. Nothing lies across a door: a house's foundation stops at its steps or porch, and a shop front's water table stops at every door.

### People in the shops

The open shops have people doing what people do there (`game/town/people.ts`, `peopleParts.ts`; five models, `client/assets/models/person.<name>.glb`, each with twelve clips, listed by hand in `people.json`). They are the drawing's, not the simulation's: no part of the plan, drawn from a hash of the building's id, the unit's id and the person's index, so the same plan gives the same people on every machine, and `TOWN_GEN` does not move. Only a unit that is open has any, and they are in the front of the room, within `FRONT_ZONE` (4 m) of the glass, which is where the room is lit and seen from the street. What each does follows the kind of room and what it holds there: staff stand behind the service point (the first checkout, counter or desk, on the long side with less clear floor to a wall, since the plan puts the counter so that the clerk sees the door) and work the till, a bartender wipes the bar, and a clerk and a customer at the counter talk about half the time; an office worker sits at a desk and types; customers of a dining room, a cafe or a counter service room sit at tables and booths and eat, and those of a bar sit on its stools, stand at a high table with a drink or lean on the pool table; in a shop they browse a shelf, rack, case or cooler, or wait at the till on the phone; in a laundromat they load a washer, fold at a table, wait on a bench or look at their phone; a workshop has someone at a bench. Where a room's service point is deeper than the front zone its staff work at something in it (stock a shelf, wipe a front table) or there is none. A standing person's spot is held to the floor (0.35 m in from the walls, 0.3 m from every furnishing, 0.4 m from a chair, outside the door's zone, 0.7 m from the next person) and never faces the glass unless the task is at something in the window; a person with no clear spot is not there. The hash also draws the model (a room repeats one only past five people), a standing person's facing up to 15 degrees off square, a height of 0.95 to 1.04, a clip rate of 0.85 to 1.1 and a start in the clip, so no two move in step.

**Where people sit is where the room is drawn with a seat.** The places chairs, stools, benches and a couch stand in are worked out in one place, `seatsOf` in `roomFill.ts` (a table's chairs, a booth's benches, a row of stools, a desk's chair, a couch, a bench, a window bar's and a high table's stools: each with its centre, the height of its top, which way a sitter faces and its family), and the drawing and `people.ts` both call it, so a sitter is on a seat that stands. A sitter's body is on the floor under the middle of the seat, raised by the seat's height less the one the clip was made for (0.46 m for a chair's clips, 0.75 m for the stool's). Seated people are not drawn on the low tier, which draws no chairs.

Each model is the engine's skinned model with its clips baked; a person is an instance with a matrix and a place in the clip of their activity, and the renderer's own clock plays them (a body that plays on costs nothing a frame). Every quarter second the people within 60 m of the eye (35 m on the low tier) are written and shown, and nothing is written in a quarter second in which the set is the same. The files are asked for once the town's geometry is made, as the vehicles' are; a model that fails to load is left out, said once. The material wears the town's air and its colour is darkened since the people stand indoors.

### Buildings from the plan

`plan.uses` (units, rooms, signs, window records) is drawn by `game/town/`, in this order for each building: the walls are cut around their openings (`buildingKit.ts` `cutWall`: a window is a hole with a reveal), each opening is filled, then the roof, the parts and the signs.

- **Fronts** (`frontGeometry.ts`). A commercial building's street wall is its units: each opening where the plan puts it (a widened one in the frame of its remodel's decade, a bricked one filled in newer brick with its lintel showing, a papered one with the paper and a corner down, a boarded one in plywood), its door where the plan puts it (recessed with returns and a step when the plan says), a bulkhead (a panel on a front of before 1960, a granite base after), pilasters between units of an old brick front, a corbelled cornice in one of four shapes by the building, the stair door, awnings (canvas in a few colours, a third faded, some torn, some only the ribs) and canopies, and the upper storeys' windows from the plan's records (some of them the vinyl of an owner's remodel).
- **Rooms** (`roomGeometry.ts`, `roomShell.ts`, `roomLight.ts`, `roomDressing.ts`, `roomKit.ts`). Behind each unit's glass the plan's room is built and lit by its own lights, baked into vertex colour. `roomLight.ts` makes a field over the room's plan (a lattice of colours, 0.75 m on the full tier): the plan's lights that are on (a tube's cool white, a pendant's and lamp's warm, neon's own colour, with a falloff and an exposure that brings the sum of a full set to one mean), a failed tube (one in about ten in a room with many tenants behind it, from the unit's record) leaving a dim patch, the day coming in through each opening (less through paper, blinds or a display) and fading with depth, a small ambient, a lamp spilling from the back room; and the furnishings' shadows on the floor. `roomShell.ts` builds the floor, ceiling and the three walls as grids of quads (about 1 m on the full tier, 2 to 5 m on the low) whose corners take the field's light (`MeshBuilder.litGrid`, glow 1: the baked light is the whole light), on the town's scanned layers where one fits (plank floors, concrete, painted plaster, panelling) tinted by the unit's own record (a vacant unit keeps its last tenant's paint, faded and stained; a seventies remodel has panelling; an old building has plank floors; the trade picks the colours), a baseboard, a wainscot or a stripe where the trade has one, and a doorway at the back (open on a lit back room or a shut door). `roomDressing.ts` hangs what each trade has on its walls (a menu board and clock in a cafe, a pegboard in a hardware store, bottles against a mirror and a lit beer sign in a tavern, a calendar and a licence in an office, a price list over the washers, rods in a tackle shop), the window's plant, the display window's goods on a low backdrop by trade, and a vacant unit's ghosts (the pale patches and brackets of shelving that came down). The furnishings (`roomGeometry.ts`) are lit by the same field at each corner: shelf runs with packages on their long faces, counters with a till, tables with chairs, booths, stools, display cases, coolers with lit fronts, washers with round doors, desks, racks, crates, a ladder, paint cans, a lift with a car. Only the faces a person outside can see are built. The low tier keeps its rooms coarse (a grid of a few quads a face, no doorway, no dressing). What stands round and on the furnishings is denser in the band too (a coat over a chair's back, a newspaper and cups on a table, clutter at a counter's street end, boxes by a tall shelf, a broom, a bucket, a hand-written sign on a stand, hours or a flyer taped inside the glass at the door's end). The back is drawn plain: the room is a tenth as bright from 6 m (`FADE_TO`) and runs to one murk, so a furnishing whose centre is deeper than `PLAIN_FROM` (6.5 m, `roomFill.ts`) is its big shape only (a table is a top on a post, a shelf run a block with a few bands of colour, no stock, chairs, backs' rails or things on tables), and its silhouette is kept because that is what gives the murk depth; a stool row keeps the stools within 5 m, which can be sat on, and `seatsOf` gives no seat to a plain furnishing, so what is drawn and what people sit on stay one thing.
- **Windows and glass** (`windowGeometry.ts`, `windowDressing.ts`, `townMaterial.ts`). Glass is a quad in a separate blended layer drawn after the opaque ones. It reflects by direction: the eye's ray is turned off the pane's normal (the wall's, tilted by a small fixed turn each pane has, more on old glass), and above the horizon the pane shows the overcast sky (paler toward the horizon), at or below it the dark, uneven band of the street's far side; how much it reflects is a floor (more for the sky) plus Schlick's fresnel, so a shop window seen from the sidewalk shows its lit room, an upper window the sky with the room faint behind it, and glass along its length is a mirror. Grime (a band at the foot, the lower corners, streaks) is laid over it from the pane's `dirt` and own `seed` (per vertex: the pane's uv, size and tilt ride in the floats a flat vertex does not use). House and upper windows are holes with trim, a sill, glass and a shallow box behind it lit by a gradient (a lamp now and then), with the plan's treatment: curtains in folds, blinds of separate slats part way down (one end sagging, a slat bent), vertical blinds, a lit room, a dark one, boards, or broken glass; a plant or a lamp on the sill, and now and then a sheet of cardboard in a pane, a window air-conditioner or a box fan. A vacant unit's paper is sheets with seams, tape, a torn corner, faded toward the top. Every layer of a front lies on a step of depth 2.5 to 3 cm from the next (the ladder: 3, 6, 9, 12 cm out from a wall; behind the glass 2.5 cm a layer), because the depth buffer (24 bits, near 0.05 m) holds two faces apart only when they are 4 steps apart at 60 m (2 cm); `test/townWorld/depthSeparation.test.ts` fails when two faces that look the same way overlap, 0.25 m^2 or more, closer than 2 cm. House and upper windows on side and back walls are the low tier's (no trim). A wall that stands within 0.7 m of another building has no opening.
- **Signs and lettering** (`shopSigns.ts`, `signStyle.ts`, `signAtlas.ts`, `signPaint.ts`). Each `Sign` is a backing of solid geometry (board, box, post, bracket) and a face: a quad in the sign layer mapped to a rectangle of a lettering atlas (4096 x 2048, 2048 x 1024 on low), a plan of shelf-packed rectangles each with a `SignSpec` (the plan's lines, style, decade, condition, lit, colours, a seed). The renderer paints the atlas with a 2D canvas (`signPaint.ts`) in slices of the build and uploads it once; with no canvas the requests are the record. The face, weight, case, layout and colours are rolled from the sign's own seed and its style and decade (`signStyle.ts`: generic font stacks with fallbacks, never a file). Kinds: fascia, banner (sagging, a corner loose), blade on a bracket, lettering on the glass (gold leaf on an arc on an old front), paint on a wall, ghost signs, poles (the gas station's price rows), sandwich boards, OPEN, FOR LEASE, civic boards. Condition fades, peels, runs rust and dirt, kills neon letters and drops those of a box, yellows plastic and cracks or removes a panel. A lit box glows evenly, lit neon only in its tubes. Small faces (a notice, the hours plate on a door, a sticker: up to 64 texels tall) are packed in a strip of their own at the foot of the atlas. Beyond the plan's signs each unit's glass carries the hours on the door (OPEN, or CLOSED and when it is back), flyers and community notices taped crooked at the edges of the glass or onto the boards of a boarded front, and stickers by the door, painted at 2 to 2.5 times the atlas's density (`windowLife`); faces that would fall on one another each stand a step in front of the last.
- **Wear by cause** (`buildingWear.ts`). The foot of a wall is a band of its own (dirt splash and algae on the shaded side), above it a wooden wall peels on the south-west faces, masonry greens on the north ones and metal rusts in streaks; dirt runs from under the eave and in a streak under each sill (a decal in the wall's material); roofs are mossy on the north slope and rust in streaks where metal. A per-building roll scales the dirt (an owner who washed the front).
- **Parts** (`buildingParts.ts`). Houses: foundation, corner boards, steps or a porch (one in six closed in), a chimney, gutters and downspouts, a meter and conduit, a heat pump or propane tank, a lean-to or carport of another siding and roof, a patch of newer siding, a patch of new roof or a tarp weighed with tires. Flat roofs: a hatch, vents, air handlers, a fire escape on old brick blocks. By kind: a gas station's canopy and pumps, a motel's covered walkway and doors, a church's bell cote (a landmark church has a steeple instead: a tower of the church's own walls entered by a pair of doors at its foot, with louvres at the bells and a shingled spire, `buildingGeometry.ts`), a fire hall's hose tower, a portico on a bank, courthouse, lodge hall, school or hotel, a warehouse's ridge vent.
- **Tiers.** The low tier draws a unit's furnishings as the six largest simple forms (no goods, chairs or garments), windows without reveal-trim or sills or a room box, no corner boards, meters, gutters, repairs or sill streaks, and a smaller atlas (36 texels a metre). Authored, not researched: every size, colour, glow, chance and roll in these files; each says in a comment which named thing a roll stands for.

### Faces the depth buffer cannot tell apart

The depth buffer is 24 bits with the near plane at 0.05 m, so a step of depth is z * z / (0.05 * 2^24) metres at a distance z (1.1 mm at 30 m, 4.3 mm at 60 m, 12 mm at 100 m, 27 mm at 150 m). Two faces that look the same way and overlap flicker as the camera moves unless they are held apart: **2 cm** (four steps at 60 m) for what is within reach of the street, **2.5 cm** above 4 m over the ground or the sea (more than one step to 145 m). Layers on a wall stand on a ladder from the wall's face: 3, 6, 9 and 12 cm (2.5 cm a layer behind glass); a sign's lettering is 3.5 cm in front of its board; a board's front is 3 cm behind the face on it and clear of any other face on the wall (`stepOff` in `shopSigns.ts`, which every sign's face, board front and banner goes through). Where two volumes meet (a box against a wall) one is sunk into the other, or the hidden face is not drawn (a quay's face stops under its cap; a run of fence along a building's wall is not drawn; a patch of paved ground is cut out under the buildings, so a room's floor is not drawn over by ground the flat layer pulls forward). What is dropped at random (a ruin's rubble, the hatch, vents and air handlers of a flat roof) is tried again clear of what is there, and given up after a few draws. `MeshBuilder.finish` leaves out a triangle that repeats one already laid with the same look, and the corners nothing uses.

`test/townWorld/depthSeparation.test.ts` holds the whole town to this (`nearPlanes.ts` finds the pairs: faces grouped over a 3 m grid, their planes compared where they overlap along the normal; `townFaces.ts` builds the town and joins pairs into patches of 25 cm^2 or more). The town's materials cull back faces (none is `doubleSided`), so faces that look opposite ways are no pair. Two flat faces of one colour are not counted (they cannot be told apart). Nothing is left: every count is 0 on five seeds on the full tier and one on the low. **The flat ground is ordered by rank, not by millimetres.** The flat layer is drawn a fixed number of depth steps ahead of the terrain (`ahead`, `townParts.ts`), which is nothing between two flat faces. Each flat vertex carries a depth rank (`RANK` in `groundSurfaces.ts`; float 11 of the vertex's plugin data), and the flat material's vertex stage draws a vertex of rank r `RANK_UNITS` * r (6 * r) steps further ahead (`townMaterial.ts`): steps of depth, so the order holds at every distance (a step is z * z / (0.05 * 2^24) m at a distance z), with no mesh and no draw added. Base surfaces are rank 0; marks, cuts, seams, cracks and paint are 1 to 5; the dark under a vehicle 7; shoulders tucked under the asphalt -1. `Surface.lay` cuts a mark off the marks of its rank (the first laid keeps its ground), so no two overlap at one rank. That step is the same number of depth units at every distance, so a flat face is pulled forward by about 1.4e-4 * z^2 metres (1.4 cm at 10 m, 13 cm at 30 m, 1.4 m at 100 m): a solid face that lies within 2.45 cm of the flat ground is held apart by the finder, and one that lies 2.5 to 5 cm over it is covered by the flat ground beyond about 100 to 200 m. The wall faces of a building (the trim, plates, frames, stickers and signs) are held 2.45 cm apart by a ledger (`faceLedger.ts`) that moves a face laid over another's place a step (3 cm) out.

### How the town is collided with

The engine's colliders are axis-aligned boxes and the old plat's grid is turned, so `townConstrain` (`sim/town/solid.ts`, the
rules' `constrain`, which runs on every machine after a player's move) collides a hull of 0.4 m radius in plan with each
building's exact oriented rectangle: out along the direction of least penetration (a corner pushes along the line from the
corner, a centre inside is taken out by the nearest side), the velocity's part into the wall removed so a walker slides, a fixed
eight passes for two walls meeting. A grid of 16 m cells, built once per world seed, names the few buildings near a place. A
building is a column from the ground to the sky; nothing reads the player's height. Buildings of no lot (the piers and the sheds
on piles) are not solid.

### Where the player starts

The plan's `spawn` is on the street side of the FBI office's door, 2 m out, facing away from the building (`port/landmarks.ts`).
`townStart(worldSeed)` (`start.ts`) is that place in world coordinates, the site's anchor plus the spawn, with its yaw. The
session's `startOf`, the world's worker and the rules' spawn all name it, so every peer agrees with nothing exchanged, and the
rules search out from it for the first place a hull stands free. The map page (`client/town.html`, `client/src/townMap/`; see
`AGENTS.md`) draws plans and a contact sheet of consecutive seeds in development, and is not part of the build.

### The railroad and the power line

The railroad's main line runs the whole coast, beside the highway on its seaward side (`rail.ts`: the highway's centre less `RAIL_OFFSET`, 16 m), and the power line runs beside the highway on its inland side. Neither begins or ends at the town: the town's own tracks leave the main line, and its streets cross it.

- **The bed** (`railGround.ts`, composed into the place's `shapeD` after the town's ground). The engine lays the highway's corridor over a place's ground within 30 m of its centre, which takes in the line, so the bed answers `staged + k (g - staged)`, `g` the grade line and `k` the bed's weight: the corridor's own weight then cancels and the ground the engine draws is `(1 - k) N + k g`. The bench is exactly the highway's grade line for that z (`RAIL_BENCH_HALF` 2.75 m either side, flat across, the grade line's slope along), falling or rising to the land over a bank whose width grows with how far the grade line stands from the land (2 m on level ground, up to 30 m, cubic ease), so a cut or a fill is never needlessly steep. The gradient is exact (tested against finite differences) and a sample farther than `RAIL_REACH` (33 m) of the line returns the town's answer as the same object after one comparison. Measured on ten seeds along 6 km: the bench differs from the grade line by under 4e-15 m; the shaping stage costs 0.07 us far from the line (as before), and 0.16 us on and beside it against 0.05 to 0.07 before, of a whole terrain sample of 1.2 us.
- **The sea** (`railSpans.ts`). Where the ground at the line's centre, as the engine would leave it with no railroad, is under 0.4 m, a run of such points (merged across gaps under 24 m) is a span, cached in chunks of 256 m so a terrain sample does not scan the coast: a causeway (the bench as fill, drawn with riprap) where it is under 2.5 m deep and 60 m long, otherwise a trestle (the ground is left, the bed's weight is taken away over 10 m at each end, and the track is carried on a deck: `railDeck.ts` makes the deck floor, like the harbour's, and holds a walker inboard of its guard rails). The natural coast does not reach the line: over 400 seeds that plan a town, along 18 km of coast each, the lowest ground at the line is 1.5 m, so no causeway or trestle stands on it; both are tested with spans laid by hand.
- **Nothing grows on it** (`railClearing.ts`, composed into the place's `featureMask`). Trees are shut within the corridor's 5 m and a tree's jitter of 3.5 m (a trunk stands up to that far from its cell's centre), opening over 3 m; ground cover within the corridor and 1.5 m, opening over 2 m; loose rock and driftwood as trees (a rock's cell is wider).
- **The track drawn** (`railGeometry.ts`, `railParts.ts`). Cells of 128 m along z, each a pure function of the seed and the cell's index, in three levels: the full cell (ballast with sloped sides and a skirt into the bench, gravel shoulders, ties every 0.55 m as boxes, rails with foot, web and head, crossings, trestles, poles with crossarms and wires), the plain one (no ties, 16 m cuts) and the far one (a ballast strip and poles, 32 m cuts). A rail's head is 0.36 m over the bench, its gauge 1.435 m, so a spur can meet it. `railParts.ts` is a part of the renderer's side that `townParts.ts` makes: each frame it wants every cell within 4300 m of the camera (the fog is 4000 m in clear weather) at the level its distance gives (full within 260 m, plain within 1300 m, with 60 m of room before a level changes), begins a job of the engine's scheduler for each (one owner a cell, at most four begun at once, nearest first, held for the engine's streamed upload budget), culls by the view's planes as the town does, and releases cells past 4700 m. At the town's start that is about 68 cells, 88 meshes, 67 thousand vertices and 33 thousand triangles (the town's own is 80 batches, 210 thousand vertices, 105 thousand triangles); the slowest cell takes under 2 ms to make.
- **Crossings** (`railCrossings.ts`). The plan finds them once (`plan.rail.crossings`, where each street and road out meets the line, a way meeting it at under about six degrees included) and `crossingsFor(plan, seed, anchor)` is that list in the world, put on the line's own centre at the plan's z: a panel carries the roadway over the rails flush with their heads on ramps either side, crossbucks stand on posts on the right of each approach, and a stop line crosses the lane that comes to the line. What is drawn is what the plan names (a test holds the two to each other for forty seeds).
- **The town's spur** (`railSpur.ts`, `spurGeometry.ts`). The plan's spur (`plan.rail.spurs`) in the world with the heights of its bed: the main line's grade line at its start, easing (over 45 m, or 40 m for each metre the made ground stands off it 100 m on) to the made ground's height along the track, held to 3.5 percent and smoothed; it ends where the bed would stand more than 2 m off the ground, and it stands on the made ground (a strip of the plane, 6 m held and 24 m of fall, laid along it in the cover, so the tideflat is level under the track). It is drawn in the town's batches as track with the main line's cross section (its ballast with a deeper skirt, ties, rails with foot, web and head at the gauge, the rail's top `RAIL_TOP` over the bed), a switch stand at the turnout, where its curve takes it off the main line (they are one track at the start, the same bed height), and a trestle (`trestleOver`, the main line's) over each stretch that crosses open water, off the fill and off the harbour's decks. A trestle's deck is ground a player walks on, `TRESTLE_TOP` over the bed at the apron's level, the top rising from the bed over 3 m at each end, with its guard rails holding a hull inboard (`spurTrestleTopAt`, `constrainTrestle`, the same mechanism as the main line's). The main line's bench and bank are laid over the town's made ground, and blocks keep clear of the bank.
- **The power line** (`powerLine.ts`). Poles every 45 m (nudged by a hash of their index) at 9 m inland of the highway's centre, moved along the line to the nearest place clear of any street's roadway; each carries a crossarm with three insulators and three wires to the next pole, as sagging ribbons. Structures only.

### Known limits

- One kind of town (the large port) and one site, at the engine's cove. The recipe structure is there for others; none is written.
- The names (the town's, the streets', the founder's, the hotel's, the mill's) are authored placeholders in ordinary English, Scots, Scandinavian and Spanish-looking words, not sourced. The building mixes and their shares are authored, and so is the order of uses along the working waterfront (lumber docks and warehouses, the terminal, the wharf street and depot, warehouses, then the canneries and the rough dock end): the research found no source for either. The history's dates and shares follow the research on Washington ports; the rest of the numbers are authored.
- The harbour is plan data and structures: boats do not move or bob, no lamp is lit, bollards, fenders, ladders and boats are not solid, a gangway is not a ramp the rules know beyond its floor, and the depth under a boat is estimated from the shore alone, so a sea stack can stand under one now and then. The mill's pond sits on the coast's shallow flats, where the logs lie near the bottom. The boat names, colours, slip classes, the yard's items and the share of terminals idle are authored, not sourced.
- No lamps and no interiors: buildings are shells, and a door is drawn, not opened. Barricades, bumpers, fences, headstones and tanks are drawn, not walls.
- The made ground holds the highway's grade line and the quay's height, so its relief is modest on the flats and rolls on the hill; its edge, where it meets the coast's own hill, fades over 160 m. A building 60 m wide on the hill's grade spans up to 4.5 m of height. The ground is still graded to a plane (4 to 8 percent on the hill) under the town: the land decides where the blocks are, within 4.5 m of the plane, and not the ground's shape under them.
- Between the highway's edge and the main line's bench the ground dips by up to half a metre (the ditch of a real line) where the highway's grade line stands over the made ground; inside the town a street crossing the line has its roadway carried over it on the crossing's panel, and the strips drape over the dip. Where the grade line stands tens of metres over the ground (a highway on a headland) the bank is steep and blocks keep 50 m off.
- A stair is judged on the coast's own ground, and the made ground under the town is graded to a plane, so in the world a stair stands on gentler ground than it was judged on (its flight is drawn at the world's heights, between its ends, and is short where the grading has flattened the slope); none is judged on the quay's fill. A few street tiles beside a rocky shore are steeper than the 8 m grid shows.
- The buildable rule's height test is 0.5 m (the coast's own flats are 0 to 2 m, and the made ground lifts them). The land is judged on the coast's own ground read at 8 m, so a stretch narrower than that is not seen. The spur crosses the cove's head from one quay end to the next shore strip over water for up to 180 m (a trestle, not drawn as one). The outlying buildings' reasons and kinds, the budget's numbers, the cost of a cell and the bay's ranges are authored, not sourced.
- Yards have few trees: a trunk keeps 3.5 m from a building, and a tree stands up to 5 m from its cell's centre, so only a yard's open middle bears one. 
- A road along the shore's flats is planned but no tested seed has room for one; the destinations are crude and stand on the ground as it is, not graded.
- The railroad: on the coast's cliffs the line, 16 m seaward of the highway and at its grade line, sometimes stands on a ledge over the land's own steep face (a fill of 9 m at the worst of ten seeds along 6 km), where the bank saturates and a wall of the land's own steepness shows; the line is drawn level across, with ties and rails that do not follow a bank. A crossing's panel spans the whole right of way and is flat shaded (no joints), its crossbucks are two boards on a post with no lights or bells, and a tie is a box that does not tilt with the grade (under 2 cm). A trestle and a causeway are built and tested but the natural coast has none. The power line's poles are straight and unlit.
- No Native places or people are generated, by decision, until they can come from the nations' own sources; no name in the generator is a Native word.
- Junctions: signals are none and no small triangular island is made at a sharp crossing (a planter closes a leg); a ramp has no flared sides; the text on a blade is capitals from a 5 by 7 pixel face, the suffix as ST, AVE or HWY; a crossing of streets within 26 degrees is not drawn as a junction but as the lesser street held back along the other's roadway, its legs closed with planters; the surface of a junction on a hillside follows the ground and so tilts with it, where WSDOT would hold 2.1 percent across a stop-controlled crossing; the highway's crosswalks lie on the engine's road at a lift tried by eye. What the research found none of is authored and said in `junctions.ts`: how two crowns meet, the gutter pan, inlets, old corners' radii, how far short of a crosswalk the centre line stops, alley widths.
- The ground: the sidewalk's slabs are planes between four corners, so on a hillside of over 6 percent across a 3 m sidewalk a slab stands a few centimetres off the ground's curve in its middle; the curb's top is drawn flat at the lots' level while the walking's ramp is 30 cm, so a person on a curb's top stands up to a few centimetres under it; the lawn near a sidewalk's back edge is cut 0 to 5 cm lower than the lot (invisible under grass) and the walker walks on its level; paved blocks (the core's concrete, the dock's gravel) are tiles of 8 m with a tint, dirt and moss each and a crack or two, not slab by slab; a sidewalk's tree-root heave follows the lot's yard kind (mossy and brush yards), not the engine's actual trunks; a street has no gutter pan or crown beyond the cut and the ground's own fall; the dark overlay's patches are the shader's noise, not painted stains.
- Props: on the low tier the vehicles are generic shapes of their type, lofted coarsely (about 700 vertices a car), so a close look shows facets (the full tier draws real models, see "Parked vehicles as models"); a code-built vehicle is drawn level on the plane of the ground under its four ends, so on a steep street its wheels can stand a few centimetres off it; the grass the engine grows is not cleared under a prop, so blades can show through a trailer or a derelict; the additions an owner made to a house (a lean-to, a carport) are drawn by `buildingParts.ts` and not known to the plan, so yard props keep 3.6 m from a house wall; piers have gear only along their edges and no vehicles but the apron's; the wires cross no street and end at a line's last pole.
- The town is an unreleased generator: `TOWN_GEN` is 19, and its digests are replaced when it changes until a first release.

## The title page

Built in `client/src/game/` in the visual language of Day Hike's title page (the same dark ground and vignette, the
monospace type, the filled and hollow buttons, the panel slide, the focus and `inert` handling), with no background still
or loop and no downloads, join box or update banner, since there is no desktop app.

- `words.ts` holds every word the page says that is the game's own, as constants.
- `landingModel.ts` decides what the page shows, as data, so the decisions are tested without a DOM: the quiet line
  `In development.` on every build while the game is that, and under it Play, `Loading…` while the world builds, or
  `Waiting for the host…` for a follower. Play is offered whether or not the build was given a signaling address: with no
  server the game is played alone, and the engine's shell shows no party.
- `landing.ts` paints the model with DOM APIs only (`textContent`, never `innerHTML`) and slides between the home panel and
  the Settings and Credits panels. Settings and Credits are the engine's screens (`renderSettings`, `renderCredits`).
- The panels have addresses, `/settings` and `/credits`, which the shell routes: `showPanel` slides the page to the panel,
  so the browser's Back closes it.
- `credits.ts` merges the repository's `CREDITS.md` (no entry yet) with the engine's `ENGINE_CREDITS`, in the order of the
  ids, then the engine's display pass.

## Hosting

The client is a static Vite build under the base `/capesorrow/` (`CAPESORROW_BASE`), published to its path on the shared
Firebase Hosting site `games.csarko.sh`, which Day Hike's repository owns. The deploy (`tools/deploy/client.mjs`) uses the
engine's shared-site tool, which merges this game's files and rules (`tools/deploy/site.rules.json`: a rewrite of every
address under `/capesorrow/` to its page, and an immutable cache header on `/capesorrow/assets/`, where Vite puts the
hashed files) into the live site without touching another game's. `tools/deploy/verify.mjs` checks the result from
outside, including that Day Hike is as it was. The map page is not in the build. There is no server deployed and no
infrastructure of this game's own, so the public page says `In development.` and Play is offered in development only.

## What is still scaffold

`client/src/{hud.ts, net.ts, assets.ts}` and `client/levels/yard.json` are the engine's empty game as its
`tools/new-game.mjs` wrote them and are unchanged: a HUD of a line of status, a snapshot format of a position and a look, the
engine's shipped trees, cliffs and sounds (the game has no shipped files of its own), and the level a forest world must carry,
with no brushes. The walking rules keep the engine's names (`WALK_*`, `EMPTY_GAME`), and `sim.ts`, `game.ts` and
`world.worker.ts`, which now carry the town, are still the scaffold's files in shape. They are to be replaced by Cape Sorrow's
own rules and parts: there is no case, no people, no sound of the game's own, and nothing to do in the town but walk. Likewise
the favicon is a placeholder, and `CREDITS.md` has no entry because the game has no third-party work of its own yet.
